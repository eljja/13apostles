### [0] High-Throughput Small Prime Pre-Sieve
```markdown
## Candidate Name
High-Throughput Small Prime Pre-Sieve

### Core Change
The current system performs expensive Miller-Rabin modular exponentiations on almost every odd candidate. The modification expands the `small_primes` list from 15 elements to all 168 primes less than 1000. This pre-check will be moved to the beginning of the primality testing logic to ensure that ~88% of composite candidates are discarded instantly via simple trial division before a single modular exponentiation is executed.

### Expected Impact
This change will drastically increase the "Candidate Throughput" (candidates tested per second). By filtering the vast majority of composites with negligible CPU cost, the organism can evaluate roughly 5x to 10x more candidates within the same 5-second window. This leads directly to a higher probability of finding larger primes and significantly boosts the Fitness score by reducing `elapsed_time` for smaller generations and increasing `best_bits` for the final generation.

### Required Cost
Minimal. The addition of 168 pre-computed integers has a negligible memory footprint and zero additional code complexity. It requires no new dependencies or specialized hardware.

### Key Risks
There is a theoretical risk of "diminishing returns" if the sieve becomes too large (e.g., checking primes up to 1,000,000), where the time spent on trial division exceeds the time saved on Miller-Rabin. However, for primes up to 1000, this risk is non-existent as modular exponentiation on large bit-sizes is orders of magnitude more expensive.

### Experiment Method
Run the organism 10 times with the baseline code and 10 times with the "High-Throughput Pre-Sieve." Compare the average `best_bits` achieved and the `total_attempts` processed within the fixed 5-second `time_limit_sec`.

### Success Criteria
1. A measurable increase in `total_attempts` (throughput) by at least 300%.
2. A higher average `best_bits` (ceiling growth) compared to the baseline.
3. Fitness score improvement of at least 2x.

### Rollback Conditions
If the `total_attempts` count decreases or the time spent per generation increases (indicating the trial division overhead is too high for the current execution environment), the system will revert to the minimal 15-prime list.
```

### [1] Adaptive Wheel Sieve Pre-filtering
```markdown
## Candidate Name
Adaptive Wheel Sieve Pre-filtering

### Core Change
Implement a trial division pre-filter using a fixed wheel of small primes (e.g., primes up to 103) before passing the candidate to the expensive Miller-Rabin test. The `make_candidate` method will be modified to ensure candidates are coprime to this set of small primes.

### Expected Impact
Significant reduction in `total_attempts` that trigger the Miller-Rabin test. Miller-Rabin is computationally heavy; eliminating obvious composites (multiples of 3, 5, 7, 11, etc.) via simple modular arithmetic reduces CPU cycles per candidate by roughly 70-80% for larger bit-sizes, directly increasing the fitness score by reducing the denominator of the cost function.

### Required Cost
- **Computational:** Negligible increase in memory (a small list of primes).
- **Development Time:** Low (~30 minutes).
- **Maintenance:** Minimal; the sieve list is static and requires no tuning.

### Key Risks
- **Complexity:** Increased logic in candidate generation might lead to a slight bias in distribution if not implemented correctly (though for primality searching, uniform distribution is secondary to finding *a* prime).
- **Implementation Error:** Incorrect wheel indexing could lead to generating numbers that are not actually odd or accidentally skipping prime candidates.

### Experiment Method
Run the modified `make_candidate` against a set of 10,000 random integers and verify that no generated candidate is divisible by any prime in the wheel. Monitor `total_attempts` vs `success_count` in a controlled 5-second trial.

### Success Criteria
A reduction of at least 50% in the total number of Miller-Rabin tests required to find a prime of the same `bit_size` compared to the current implementation.

### Rollback Conditions
- If the number of primes found within the 5-second window decreases.
- If the CPU time spent in `make_candidate` exceeds the time saved by skipping Miller-Rabin rounds.
```

### [2] Proposal from Apostle 3
```markdown
## Small-Prime Pre-filtering Extension

### Core Change
Expand the `small_primes` list within the `is_probable_prime` function from its current 15 entries (primes up to 47) to the first 100 primes (primes up to 541). The loop will then perform trial division against this expanded list before proceeding to the Miller-Rabin rounds.

### Expected Impact
This change significantly improves **Discovery Speed** and **Efficiency**. Trial division with small primes is computationally "cheap" compared to the modular exponentiation required by the Miller-Rabin test. By filtering out ~88% of odd composite numbers using the first 100 primes, the organism avoids wasting expensive CPU cycles on candidates that are obviously composite. This allows more candidates to be checked within the 5-second `time_limit_sec`, increasing the probability of finding larger primes and thus raising the overall `fitness`.

### Required Cost
Development time is minimal (less than 5 minutes to generate/verify the prime list). Computational cost is a negligible increase in memory for the integer list and a slight increase in linear comparisons for candidates that survive the sieve, which is heavily offset by the reduction in modular exponentiation calls.

### Key Risks
The risk is nearly zero. As long as the integers in the expanded `small_primes` list are actually prime, the logic remains mathematically sound. There is a theoretical point of diminishing returns where the sieve time exceeds the saved Miller-Rabin time, but with only 100 primes and bit-sizes doubling from 16, we are far from that limit.

### Experiment Method
The modified organism will be run 10 times in a controlled environment. The results will be compared against 10 runs of the original `0.py`. We will record the average `total_attempts` and the average `best_bits` achieved within the fixed 5-second lifetime.

### Success Criteria
The experiment is successful if the modified code achieves a higher average `fitness` score than the baseline and reaches a `best_bits` value equal to or greater than the baseline in at least 80% of the trials.

### Rollback Conditions
Immediate rollback if the code fails to identify known primes (logic error) or if the `elapsed_time` per candidate discovery increases for bit-sizes above 128 (indicating the sieve overhead has become counter-productive).
```

### [3] Tiered Trial-Division Pre-filtering
```markdown
## Candidate Name
Tiered Trial-Division Pre-filtering

### Core Change
Decouple the initial divisibility check from the `is_probable_prime` function and expand it into a dedicated `pre_filter` stage. The current list of 15 small primes will be expanded to the first 128 primes (up to 719). The candidate generation loop will be modified to only pass candidates that survive this "cheap" trial division to the "expensive" Miller-Rabin test.

### Expected Impact
This change addresses scalability at high bit-lengths. As `bit_size` increases (e.g., 1024 to 2048+ bits), the computational cost of Miller-Rabin (modular exponentiation) grows cubically, whereas trial division grows linearly. By filtering out ~82% of composite candidates using negligible CPU cycles, the system can scale its search 5-10x more efficiently, allowing it to reach significantly higher bit-sizes within the 5-second lifetime.

### Required Cost
- **Computational:** Very low. A one-time definition of a list of 128 integers and a simple loop of modulo operations.
- **Development:** Minimal. Refactoring the existing `small_primes` check into a separate function.
- **Maintenance:** Low. The prime list is static and requires no updates.

### Key Risks
- **Overhead at Small Bit-sizes:** For very small `bit_size` (e.g., 16-bit), the time spent checking 128 primes might slightly exceed the time saved by skipping Miller-Rabin.
- **False Negatives:** If the prime list includes an even number or is incorrectly typed, it could filter out valid primes (though extremely unlikely given the simplicity).

### Experiment Method
Run the organism 100 times in the current environment to establish a baseline for `best_bits` and `fitness`. Implement the Tiered Pre-filtering and run another 100 trials. Compare the average `best_bits` reached and the `total_attempts` per success ratio.

### Success Criteria
- **Primary:** An increase in the average `best_bits` discovered within the 5-second window.
- **Secondary:** A higher `fitness` score, specifically driven by a reduction in `elapsed_time` per successful prime discovery in the higher bit-ranges (512 bits and above).

### Rollback Conditions
- If the average `elapsed_time` for finding 16-bit and 32-bit primes increases by more than 20% (indicating the sieve overhead is too high for small-scale operations).
- If no primes are found within the time limit for three consecutive runs (indicating a logic error in the filter).
```

### [4] Proposal from Apostle 5
```markdown
## Time-Bounded Defensive Primality Test

### Core Change
Modify the `is_probable_prime` function and its call site to implement strict temporal and input guardrails. Specifically:
1.  **Temporal Guard:** Add a `deadline` parameter to `is_probable_prime`. Inside the Miller-Rabin witness loop, insert a check: `if time.time() > deadline: return False`.
2.  **Input Guard:** Add an explicit check for `n <= 4` and `n % 2 == 0` at the start of the function to prevent potential `ValueError` crashes in `random.randrange(2, n - 2)` and to bypass unnecessary computation for even candidates.
3.  **Call Site Update:** In `search_one_generation`, calculate the remaining lifetime (`self.birth_time + self.time_limit_sec`) and pass it as the `deadline`.

### Expected Impact
This change ensures "graceful degradation" of the organism's search capability. As bit-sizes grow and the computational cost of Miller-Rabin increases cubically, the organism will no longer "hang" or exceed its 5-second budget during a single, long primality test. This guarantees that the system always preserves enough resources to complete its lifecycle, report final statistics, and calculate fitness accurately without being killed by the environment's hard timeout.

### Required Cost
Minimal computational cost. Each primality test will perform one additional floating-point comparison (`time.time()`) per round. Development time is low, requiring only a few lines of logic.

### Key Risks
The primary risk is a "False Negative" where a prime number is flagged as composite because the deadline expired mid-test. However, in a robustness context, a safe false negative is preferable to an uncontrolled resource overrun or a process hang.

### Experiment Method
Run the organism with a modified `starting_bit_size` of 2048 and a 1.0s `time_limit_sec`. Compare the `elapsed_time` of the original code (which may exceed 1s) against the hardened version.

### Success Criteria
1.  **Resource Adherence:** The `elapsed_time` at the end of the organism's life must not exceed `time_limit_sec` by more than 50ms (account for OS jitter).
2.  **Zero Crashes:** No `ValueError` or `InternalError` should be raised regardless of the candidate `n` passed to the test.

### Rollback Conditions
1.  If the overhead of checking the system clock reduces the `total_attempts` by more than 10% for bit-sizes under 512.
2.  If the deadline logic causes an infinite loop or logical failure in prime discovery.
```
